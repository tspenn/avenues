**MEMO**

To: Stephen Miller  
From: The Editor, Avenues  
Date: 19 September 2026  
Re: Getting the pump down before November — a solidarity week, Patriot Stations, and a groundbreaking

Mr. Miller,

The object is the price on the corner: a lower gallon that Americans can see now, and can still see on November 3.

What follows is a proposal rather than a finished plan, and it works with what already exists — pumps that are already pouring and retailers who are already looking for a reason to be liked. It asks for two weeks of concentrated effort aimed at a single number, plus one move on day one that is not written down here, and one announcement aimed well past November that nonetheless pays before it.

**The Problem**

As of this morning the national average for regular is $4.48 a gallon. It was $4.44 on Thursday, sixteen cents above the week before and a dollar twenty-four above where it stood a year ago. We are now within a dime of this year's high, which was $4.56 in May, and the all-time record of $5.01 is no longer comfortably far off.

The number that ought to be leading every conversation, though, is diesel. On 17 September it reached $6.40, and that is not merely a bad week — it is the highest price diesel has ever carried in the United States. A year ago it was $3.71, which means the cost of moving anything in this country has risen by roughly seventy-four percent in twelve months. Crude has been holding above $100 a barrel on the back of the Strait, and refining margins have widened alongside it.

Diesel is where the pain concentrates and where it multiplies. It lands first on the people who haul, who farm, who run a shop that empties a tank before lunch. Then it lands on everyone else a second time, because groceries ride on diesel and the checkout tells that story about a week later. A family driving an hour each way feels the commute, and then feels the cart.

That sign will still be standing on November 3 unless something pulls it down.

Part of the explanation offered for it is real. The Strait is genuinely contested and stocks are genuinely tight. But part of it is margin taken while the price is described from a podium as reasonable, and no rally is equipped to audit the difference. What might actually move the board is pressure applied from more than one direction at once.

**Week One — Conservation as Solidarity**

This is not a lifestyle lecture and it must never be allowed to sound like one. It is a demand test, run in public, at a moment when the country is already paying attention to the pump.

The ask goes out from a Rose Garden podium on the Monday afternoon, delivered by the Vice President, or by the President if he wants the clip:

> Stand with the Iranians who died this year asking for the same freedom we were handed. What we are asking of you is not a hard sacrifice. Shorten the extra trip. Carpool where you can. Do the week's shopping in one store instead of three.

Then everyone looks at the sign.

One week, measured on AAA regular and diesel, Monday to Monday. No thermostats, no transition vocabulary, and above all no promise of a two-dollar gallon that the crude market will not permit.

A word on the downside, which I would rather put in writing than leave to be found later. This week may not move the pump at all, and the plan should be built to survive that. If gallons fall and the price eases, households get a lower number and the administration gets the credit for having asked. If gallons fall and the price does not move, that is worth knowing and worth saying out loud, because it tells you the pump is not tracking American demand. And if nobody changes their driving at all, the civic ask is retired quietly after seven days while the rest of the work continues. A failed carpool should not be marched into week four.

**Week Two — Patriot Stations**

This is announced publicly, from the podium, once week one has produced a number — including an unflattering one.

Nobody receives advance notice of it, and there is no private version of the offer. The terms are published once and they are identical for everyone: cut your price by the posted amount, and a published share of every gallon you sell at that price is donated in your station's name.

That is the whole of it, and it is open to anyone. A single pump on a county road can sign. So can a major, if a major wants to. There is no list of who was asked, because nobody was asked — the invitation is simply standing in public, and joining it is a decision each owner makes for himself. That is what makes it a patriot's program rather than a deal.

What the owner gets back is the listing, and the listing is the whole of the compensation. There is no subsidy here and no check from the government. A station that posts the Patriot price goes onto the map in the Patriot Stations app, and that map is what sends a driver eleven miles out of his way to buy from the man who signed instead of the one on the interstate who did not.

That is a real trade rather than a favor being asked. A few cents given up on the gallon buys traffic through the door, and at a convenience store or a truckstop the gallon was never where the money was — it is the coffee, the food, the ticket rung up inside by a driver who came for the diesel. The independents understand this arithmetic considerably better than anyone in Washington does, because it is how they have survived next to the majors for forty years. All we are doing is giving the whole country a reason to look up their address.

Free to sign and free to stay. A sign for the window, a name from the podium, and a place on the map. The app carries the location of every participating site, the price posted there, and the running tally of what those gallons have put into children's accounts. When an owner takes the price back off the board, the listing comes off with him, and that is the only enforcement this program ever needs.

One point about the app that matters more than it sounds. It is not an application in the sense that requires anyone's permission. It opens from a link in the phone's browser and saves to the home screen with a single tap — nothing to download, no store, no review, no approval. That is a deliberate decision rather than a technical shortcut.

Anything carrying the President's name that lives inside the Apple and Google stores also lives at the pleasure of two review committees, either of which can take it down in the last week of October and is under no obligation to explain why. That is not a hypothetical; it has been done to others, and the appeal takes longer than the election would. Served from an address we own, it can be reached by anyone with a phone and removed by nobody.

It also travels further this way than a store listing ever would. A link is something a person can paste into a post, and whoever taps it is looking at the map a second later — no download, no account, no permissions screen, none of the four separate steps at which an ordinary person gives up and closes the phone. This program will be carried by the people who like it, and what they will be handing each other is an address that works the instant it opens.

What it does is plain enough. It asks the handset where it is and shows the Patriot Stations nearby with the price each one is posting today. The location stays on the phone and is used to draw the map rather than collected, and no account is needed to open it. A driver looking for cheaper diesel should not have to register with anyone to find it.

The discount hangs on diesel first. Regular is welcome, but only from operators willing to cut both, because diesel is where the record is and where a cut will actually be felt. The number has to be set from real supply rather than from a speech, and it has to be low enough that a driver who just paid wartime diesel notices it before he finishes reading the board.

That figure is not mine to set and it should not be set in this building alone. It comes out of conversations with the people who actually own the pumps — SIGMA and NACS for the independent marketers, NATSO for the truckstops, where the diesel volume genuinely lives. The cut has to survive their margins before it ever reaches a podium, because a number that puts an owner underwater is a number nobody posts twice.

The owner keeps the deed and keeps a living margin, or nobody signs and the program is a press release. The old name stays on the building. Patriot is the board and the contract, not a government chain — there is no chain, there is a list, and a station joins that list when its pump is genuinely selling the discount. Ten stations is ten stations. The list grows when the next independent signs with the distributor, and each new site gets named locally and named again at the next rally.

Stations that stay off the list keep the wartime board. They do not get the sign and they do not get the children's tally. Nobody is being boycotted; we are simply building the only gallons in America that cut diesel and fund a child at the same time.

On the children's side, the road is already paved and someone else laid it.

Treasury seeds a thousand dollars into the account of every eligible child born since the start of 2025. Michael and Susan Dell committed six and a quarter billion dollars in December to put two hundred and fifty into the accounts of roughly twenty-five million children who fell outside that window. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley both matched the federal seed for their employees' children in July, and by August companies were signing the Invest America Business Pledge to contribute on a standing annual basis. Dozens of employers have programs running now.

The accounts belong to individual children and open only when a parent claims one, which is precisely the friction the Dell gift was built to overcome — that money was offered as a reason to go and claim the account. The same logic works here, and it works harder, because the reason is standing on the corner in numbers a foot tall.

If we gave once, we can give twice. Running the Patriot contribution through the existing nonprofit channel rather than building something new has the additional virtue of keeping it structurally separate from any campaign account, so that nobody has to promise the separation and be believed.

What makes this one different is where the money comes from. Every contributor so far wrote a check against a corporate budget. This one comes out of gallons sold cheap to Americans who needed them, which is a better story than any of them, and it runs on the same plumbing.

The legal work on the trustee and the distributor agreements can begin now, and so can the app, which has to be finished and live at its own address before the President says its name out loud. A listing nobody can find is not compensation, and the first owner who cuts his price into an empty map will tell every other owner in his state about it.

The public name and the first live boards wait for week two. Addresses go up as they sign, and every participating site stays lit through November, because the duration is the program. A video of one station on one afternoon is not.

**Week Two — The Ground Gets Broken**

Everything above is a fortnight of work aimed at a price. The objection to all of it is the same objection, and it will be made the morning after the first Patriot board goes up: none of this fixes why the number is high.

That objection is correct. There is an answer to it, and the answer can be started before November.

The United States has not built a new refinery since 1977. The last one of any size broke ground in 1973, in Garyville, Louisiana, and Gerald Ford was president when it lit. We ran 205 refineries in 1990 and we run 130 now. Capacity has fallen better than four percent since January of 2020, and three plants have shut in the last eighteen months — one of which took five percent off the entire West Coast, a region with almost no pipeline reaching it from the Gulf.

That is the real reason a single power failure at one plant in Illinois on a Sunday afternoon put diesel up sixty-six cents in Michigan within five days. There is no slack left anywhere in the system. The Strait is real, but the Strait is not why one outage moves four states.

So: a refinery built as a public work by the Army Corps of Engineers, under emergency authority. It was reported on 11 September that this building is already examining refining capacity through the Defense Production Act. The instrument is sitting there.

I will be straight about the timing, because somebody will raise it within a day of your reading this. It does not produce a gallon before November, and nothing can make it.

What it produces before November is a groundbreaking.

I would argue that is the most valuable single image available to this administration between now and the third. Every other answer to the price is a promise about next week, and the country has been handed a great many of those and has learned what they are worth. A shovel in the ground is not a promise. It is a site, a date, a payroll, and a photograph that runs in every market in the country, and it says the one thing an opponent cannot answer — that somebody finally started.

The standard estimate for a refinery is eight to ten years, and that estimate is old. Very little of that decade was ever concrete. It was design, permitting, procurement and sequencing — coordination work, which is precisely the category whose cost has collapsed in the last three years. The Corps is not a firm that rents engineers project by project the way an oil company does; it is thirty-seven thousand of them already on the federal payroll, organized into districts that do nothing else. And the heavy forging bottleneck that used to make the pressure vessels a ten-year item by itself now has a path around it: ASME has approved the code case for additively manufactured pressure parts, with no strength penalty at all where the finished part is fully inspected.

None of that makes it fast in the ordinary sense of the word. It makes it exponentially faster than 1973, which is the only comparison anybody has. We laid twelve hundred and fifty miles of pipeline from Texas to Pennsylvania in three hundred and fifty days in 1942, with slide rules, while fighting two wars on two oceans. That belongs in the speech.

One caution on sequencing, and it is the reason this belongs in week two and not week one. The industry will read a federal refinery as a threat if it arrives before the courtesy calls, and Monday morning is the whole of your goodwill with them. Afterward it reads differently — particularly if the operating contract goes out to bid. The Corps builds; it does not run a catalytic cracker and it should never be asked to. Government-owned and contractor-operated is how this country has run shipyards, arsenals and laboratories for a century, and it means the majors are not the target of the announcement. They are candidates to operate it.

That is the distance between a punishment and a contract, and saying it that way costs nothing.

The case underneath is not ideological, which is what makes it hard to argue with. At record margins, in the year diesel crossed six dollars, the three largest refiners in the country each reported capacity increases of under one percent. That is not an accusation; it is in their own filings. Their caution is rational — nobody commits ten billion dollars to a forty-year asset while half the political system has promised to legislate it worthless. But rational or not, it means this does not get built by the market, and has not been for forty-nine years. A country that cannot refine what it pumps is not energy independent. It is one power failure away from a bad month, and we have just had the power failure.

There is one more argument here, and it is the one I would most want made to the industry — though not on Monday, and not by anyone standing in a government building.

Nothing has ever sold an electric car in this country like four dollars and forty-eight cents. Not the mandates, not the credits, not fifteen years of being lectured at. A decade of deliberate policy could not move the American driver off gasoline, and one bad autumn at the pump is doing it free of charge. You can hear it now in rooms where it has never been said before — a man in a rally crowd who came out to boo the Green New Deal, turning to the person beside him and saying that Big Oil is making him want an electric car.

That sentence ought to frighten the refiners considerably more than any committee hearing does, because it is not coming from their opposition. It is coming from the last constituency they have.

The pump is the best advertising the electric car has ever gotten, and the industry is paying for the entire campaign itself. Nobody has to say that to them out loud. They can hear it on the evening news along with everyone else, and they can do the arithmetic faster than we can.

The name is yours. Big Beautiful Refinery will write itself on a rally floor, and there is no reason to be shy about it.

**How It Stacks**

Monday morning, the folders. Monday afternoon, the Rose Garden asks the country for seven days of small sacrifices and everyone begins watching the sign together. Week two, Patriot Stations goes live with independents, a distributor, discounted diesel, and a children's contribution drawn only from those gallons, open to anyone willing to post the price. Then the ground gets broken on the first American refinery in forty-nine years, which answers the question the other two cannot. November sits on top of all of it.

Three things, and they do different work. The first two are aimed at the number on the corner between now and the third. The third is aimed at the reason that number is what it is, and its whole value before the election is the photograph of the shovel.

**What I Would Not Do**

I would not put children's money anywhere near a campaign account, or leave that separation to good intentions when a legal structure can do it instead.

I would not promise a national two-dollar average that the crude market will not permit.

I would not take the first Patriot board down the Monday after a good headline.

I would not let the refinery get announced ahead of the folders, because it is the one piece of this that can be heard as a threat, and it only has to be heard that way once.

And I would not put the folders in writing, which is why they are not in here.

**In One Sentence**

Get the pump down before November with the folders on Monday morning, a light week of solidarity from Monday afternoon, then Patriot Stations — independents on a new distributor, diesel discounted where the record actually sits, and a children's contribution drawn only from those gallons — and break ground on the first American refinery since 1977, because the country deserves to be told why the number got this high and shown that somebody has finally started on it.
