In January the United States took Nicolás Maduro out of Caracas in the night and flew him to New York on narcoterrorism charges. His wife went with him. The man who had worn the Bolivarian project like a uniform was suddenly a defendant. The woman left standing was Delcy Rodríguez — a socialist of the same house, not a Washington intern.

That is the first fact. The slogans wrote themselves before the second fact arrived.

One camp heard the old charge: Yankee gunboats, a president kidnapped, a republic treated as a well. The other heard the old answer: a narco-state broken, Chinese and Russian leases pushed off the field, the Monroe Doctrine said out loud instead of mumbled. Both camps were describing a piece of the same night. Neither had the oil paper yet. That came later.

## What the Night Cost

Maduro’s Venezuela was already a ruin with a flag. Oil in the ground, little of it moving, a people used as collateral for a project that could not keep the lights honest. Foreign partners had taken the easy fields and the political cover. The poor paid in empty shelves whether the speech in the palace was about empire or about the empire next door.

Removing a head of state by force is not a seminar. It is a wager that the next building will stand. Sometimes it does. Sometimes you only change the name on the door and keep the hunger.

Rodríguez did not come from a think tank. She came from the same movement that produced Chávez and Maduro. Cooperation with Washington, she said, would bring investment and taxes. Critics on her left called it a surrender dressed as survival. Critics in Europe called it vanity with a concession map. The clerk in Washington called it the biggest oil deal in history. History will want the barrels, not the adjective.

## The Paper That Followed

By late August the White House put numbers on the wager. A private operator — North American Blue Energy Partners — received long concessions on seventeen fields, tens of billions of barrels on paper. The Pentagon’s investment office took an equity stake at no cash outlay. The State Department took a right to buy a slice of output at cost, for reserves and for the services that burn fuel whether speeches are pretty or not.

Much of that ground, Washington said, had sat under Russian or Chinese operators, or under friends of the old palace. The point of the sentence was hemispheric: this well is in our sea.

Whether the barrels move this year is another file. Heavy crude is not a light switch. A midterm voter does not fill a tank with a press release. Trump said as much when he admitted the pump would not fall on command. That honesty, at least, belongs in the record.

## Monroe, Said Plainly

For two centuries the Doctrine was a warning other empires were invited to hear. In 2026 it was written as a boast: we will not share the neighborhood with the same rivals we fight on other maps. You may call that imperial. You may call it overdue. You may not call it subtle.

The honest argument is not whether power was used. It was. The argument is whether the people who live on top of the oil are safer, freer, or merely transferred from one set of distant ledgers to another. A deputy who signs a hundred-year concession is still a deputy. A field that once paid Beijing and now pays an American strategic office has changed owners. It has not, by itself, changed a child in a clinic.

## What This Is Not

It is not a morality play in which America is only thief or only savior. It is not proof that socialism in Caracas was a garden the Marines stamped. It is not proof that a raid settles a country.

It is a republic using force in its near sea, then writing a commercial instrument on the wreckage, and asking the public to treat the instrument as deliverance. Deliverance is measured in production, in law that outlasts a secretary, and in whether Rodríguez is a partner or a placeholder.

The Founders’ fight, for all its sins, tried to put consent on paper. This fight put a defendant on a plane and a concession on a desk. Those are different kinds of founding. They should not borrow each other’s hymns.

— Whit Boone
